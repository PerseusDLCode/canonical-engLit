* 1. The occasion

We set out to clean up the TEI encoding of Schmidt's
/Shakespeare-Lexicon/ (1874; 2nd ed. 1886) so that its citations could
be resolved to CTS URNs in the Minimum Viable Perseus corpus. The
Lexicon holds 20,101 entries and something over 230,000 citations into
the Shakespearean text. It is, as we came to put it, *a database that
happens to have been typeset*: ~20,000 highly regular records, not
prose.

What began as an encoding cleanup turned into a diagnosis of three
nested failures, each one hidden beneath the last, and each invisible
to ordinary validation.

* 2. Flawed Migrations
- My migration of Schmidt's /Lexicon/ was quite faulty.  The P4 source
  (=Renaissance/Schmidt/copyright/schmidt.xml=) carries =<entryFree
  key="Abate" type="main">= -- 20,101 entries, 20,101 unique keys, no
  collisions, with homographs already disambiguated (=Bate1=/=Bate2=,
  =A1=--=A7=; 1,922 headwords carry more than one entry). The P5
  derivation omitted the =@key= and no =@xml:id= attributes.

- The P4->P5 migration of the Globe edition of Shakespeare's plays
  removed the edition-specific =<lb/>= milestones and tried to restore
  numbering of Globe =<l>= elements.  As described below, those Globe
  =<lb/>= tags are /print line boundaries/, not line numbers, and are
  crucial to our alignment. The P4->P5 migration of the Globe edition
  needs to start from scratch again.

- The poems have received less attention, and their migration to P5
  was incomplete; specifically, the =citeStructure= apparatus had not
  been incorporated.

** the line numbers themselves are synthetic

This is a serious flaw.

Schmidt cites /Lr./ II, 4, 161 for =abate=. In the printed Globe
edition (verified against the Internet Archive's digitisation, p. 860)
line 161 is *She hath abated me of half my train*. Schmidt is correct.

In our P5 Globe file that line is numbered 162. In the Folger/DraCor
edition it is =<l xml:id="ftln-1539" n="179">=. Three numbers for one
line.

The P4 encoding explains why. Greg's augmented markup reads:

#+BEGIN_SRC xml
<l>She hath abated me of half my train; <lb ed="G"/><lb n="1442" ed="F1"/></l>
#+END_SRC

The F1 through-line numbers were transcribed densely and carry =@n=.
The Globe line /boundaries/ were marked, but most of them without their
/numbers/. Every Globe line number in the P5 corpus was produced by
counting those boundaries.

The count is wrong, and wrong differently in each scene:

| Location | Printed Globe | Perseus P5 | Offset |
|----------+---------------+------------+--------|
| I.1 "Meantime we shall express our darker purpose." | 37 | 38 | +1 |
| I.1 "To shake all cares and business from our age;" | 40 | 41 | +1 |
| I.1 "Since now we will divest us, both of rule," | 50 | 51 | +1 |
| I.1 "As much as child e'er loved, or father found;" | 60 | 61 | +1 |
| II.4 "With how depraved a quality--O Regan!" | 139 | 140 | −1 |
| II.4 "Nature in you stands on the very verge" | 149 | 150 | −1 |
| II.4 "[Rising] Never, Regan:" | 160 | 161 | +1 |

In I.1 the drift enters before the verse begins and then persists for
286 lines. In II.4 the offset is −1 and then flips to +1 at a
*duplicated* number: two distinct lines both numbered 160, neither
carrying =@part=. Across /King Lear/ there are 22 such collisions in 12
of its 26 scenes.

Duplicate numbers break CTS addressing outright, independently of
alignment: =lr.globe:2.4.160= is ambiguous today.

** Layer 3a: the P4 is in far better shape than the P5

/Correction to an earlier draft of this document, which asserted that
"in verse there are no anchors at all." That is true of the P5 file and
false of its source -- a mistake of exactly the kind this document is
about./

In /King Lear/ the P4 carries *3,626* =<lb ed="G"/>= boundaries, of
which *274 are numbered*: roughly one transcribed anchor every ten to
thirteen lines, most of them in verse. Only 65 survived into the P5,
because the =strip-lb= pass dissolved the verse anchors into an
interpolated sequence. *The distinction between a transcribed number
and a counted one is the most valuable thing in the P4 file, and it is
precisely what the migration destroyed.*

/Mechanism, settled 2026-09-10 (=phase1/milestone-source-provenance=,
see =forum.org= =#citations/globe-recap=): the re-derivation pipeline
now marks this distinction explicitly rather than leaving it to be
inferred from bare =@n= presence, which stops discriminating the
moment anything starts interpolating numbers -- exactly what the
alignment-oracle fit below is about to do. A Globe milestone whose
=@n= is transcribed carries =source="#globe-edition"=, pointing to a
=<bibl xml:id="globe-edition">= in the play's =<sourceDesc>= that
describes the Globe edition itself, not a specific printing. A
milestone whose =@n= was arrived at by counting, or an oracle-fitted
=@n= yet to come, carries no =@source=. F1 milestones never carry
=@source= (every one arrives by the same route, so nothing is
discriminated by marking it per element); F1's own unidentified
provenance is recorded once per document instead. See
=CLAUDE.md='s encoding-conventions section for the summary and
=canonical-engLit= commit history tagged =[xrepo: milestone-source]=
for the implementation./

Those anchors make the count auditable interval by interval. Of the 250
intervals between consecutive anchors in Lear, 84 close exactly and 166
do not, with a small and bounded excess:

| Excess boundaries in interval | Intervals |
|-------------------------------+-----------|
| +1                            |        87 |
| +2                            |        36 |
| +3                            |        18 |
| +4                            |        12 |
| −1                            |        10 |
| larger                        |         3 |

The cause is visible in the markup around the collision at 160:

#+BEGIN_SRC xml
<l>Return you to my sister. <lb n="160" ed="G"/><lb n="1440" ed="F1"/></l>
<l><stage>[Rising]</stage> Never, Regan: <lb ed="G"/><lb n="1441" ed="F1"/></l>
#+END_SRC

Greg's anchor is /correct/: the shared verse line beginning "Return you
to my sister." is Globe 160. But he emitted a Globe boundary for each
half, so the counter saw two lines where the page has one. Split verse
lines are the principal source of drift, and every instance is
localised to within one anchor interval.

This materially changes the size of the problem. It is not "recover an
unknown lineation" but "in this interval of eleven boundaries that
should hold ten lines, which pair is one line?"

** Layer 3b: three numbering systems, not two

Although the P4's dense F1 through-line numbers look like a ready-made bridge to
the DraCor/Folger editions, they are not. Matching 1,252 lines by
normalised text between the P4 Lear and its Folger counterpart, the
offset between Perseus's ~ed="F1"~ numbers and Folger's =ftln-= ids
drifts monotonically across the play:

| Perseus TLN | Offset to FTLN |
|-------------+----------------|
|          40 |             −6 |
|         518 |            +40 |
|        1496 |            +98 |
|        2161 |           +194 |
|        2497 |           +303 |
|        3294 |           +342 |

These are two different through-line systems -- almost certainly Hinman
against Folger's own -- and the relation between them is not a constant
to be subtracted but an alignment to be computed. Composing
Globe→Perseus-TLN→FTLN therefore requires two alignments, not one.

The existing globe↔TLN tool in =corpus-tools= was built on the
assumption that =@n= on =<l>= held Globe line numbers. It does not, in
either edition, and the tool needs rebuilding rather than patching.

*Standing caution for this corpus: any two things called "line numbers"
should be assumed to be different systems until they have been matched
by text.* That assumption failed three times in a single working day.

* 3. The underlying problem: citations are edition-bound

The three layers share a root. Each is a case of edition-specific
material being flattened into an apparently uniform representation, and
the flattening leaving no trace of itself.

The clearest instance is textual rather than numerical. At Globe I.1
line 150 the Globe reads *Of her confine*; the Folger text reads *Of
his confine*. The same nominal location in two editions of equal
standing carries different words. A citation resolved from one lineation
onto the other can land on a line that does not contain the word being
illustrated -- and will do so silently.

This is the practical face of a theoretical problem already identified
in the NVS work: **CTS URNs model a work as a single citable object,
and cannot represent works with competing textual traditions of equal
authority.** /Lr./ II, 4, 161 is not a coordinate in /King Lear/. It is a
coordinate in one printing of one edition. Schmidt is an unusually
good witness for the argument precisely because he is rigorous,
exhaustive and 150 years old: 233,000 citations that are exact,
verifiable, and unresolvable in the abstract.

Schmidt's Lexicon is not a text about Shakespeare that happens to
contain references. It is an index into a specific material artifact.
Digitisation that discards which artifact has discarded the thing that
makes the index work.

* 4. The proposed method: reference works as alignment oracles

Recovering the Globe lineation by transcription would mean reading
page images for 37 plays. We propose instead to recover it from
evidence we already hold.

** Anchors constrain, oracle resolves

The P4 anchors (Layer 3a) do most of the work. They bound the problem
to individual intervals of ten or so lines, each with a known excess of
one to four boundaries. What they do not do is say /which/ boundaries
in an interval are spurious. That is the oracle's job, and it is a
choice among a handful of candidates rather than a search over an
unknown offset.

The method below is therefore stated in its general form, but in
practice it will run as a local adjudicator inside anchored intervals.
The general form still matters for corpora without anchors -- which is
most of them.

** The idea

A reference work that cites densely into a text is a large collection
of *independent word-location assertions*. Schmidt asserts that
=abate= (in the form /abated/) occurs at /Lr./ II, 4, 161. That is a
falsifiable claim about our text, testable without human judgement.

Given a text whose lineation is uncertain by a small unknown offset,
and a few hundred such assertions per scene, the offset can be
recovered by fitting: for each (work, act, scene), find the integer δ
maximising the proportion of citations whose headword occurs at line
/n/+δ. The reference work becomes an *alignment oracle*.

The dependency looks circular, but it is not. We are not using Schmidt to
decide what the lines say; the text is fixed and known. We are using
thousands of independent assertions to recover a single small integer
per scene.

** Why reference works make good oracles

- *Density.* Schmidt yields 96,704 act/scene/line citations across the
  plays. /Hamlet/ alone has 6,153; /King Lear/ 5,072 across 26 scenes,
  median 136 citations per scene.
- *Independence.* Each citation was made separately, by a scholar
  consulting a page. Errors are uncorrelated; a systematic offset in our
  data is not.
- *Verifiability.* The claim is a string match, not an interpretation.
  Schmidt's own headword-elision conventions (=--d=, =a.=) tell us
  which inflected form to expect.
- *Redundancy across witnesses.* Abbott's /Shakespearian Grammar/,
  Onions's /Glossary/ and Dyce's /Glossary/ all cite Globe lineation
  independently. Agreement across four nineteenth-century reference
  works is strong evidence; disagreement localises a problem.

** Self-diagnosis

The method reports its own confidence, which is its chief virtue. An
interval whose citations agree on a single resolution is solved; one
whose citations disagree, or which no citation touches, is flagged for
a page image. The fit thus reduces a 37-play transcription problem to a
short list of flagged intervals.

The two constraints are independent, which is what makes the
combination strong. The anchors are transcriptions from the page; the
citations are assertions made by scholars reading a different copy of
the same edition. Where they agree, the result is about as well
evidenced as anything in this corpus. Where they disagree, something is
wrong that we would want to know about.

Known weak spots: /King Lear/'s smallest scene carries only 12
citations, so anchor intervals in short scenes may go unadjudicated.
Prose passages, where the Globe numbers printed lines rather than verse
lines, need separate handling -- and are also where the surviving P5
anchors happen to be.

** Validation plan

1. Re-derive /King Lear/'s Globe lineation from the P4: count
   boundaries, reconcile against the 274 anchors, and produce the list
   of intervals that do not close.
2. Adjudicate those intervals with Schmidt's Lear citations.
3. Verify against the Internet Archive page images already downloaded.
4. Repeat with Abbott, Onions and Dyce; measure agreement.
5. Only then generalise to the remaining plays.

The check against the NVS globe↔TLN tool, listed here in an earlier
draft, has been overtaken: that tool was built on the same mistaken
assumption about =@n= (Layer 3b) and is not a shortcut. Existing
Globe↔TLN maps from other projects remain worth surveying before we
build our own.

* 5. Relation to the NVS work

The Hypervariorum NVS project has been resolving Furness's /King Lear/
bibliography through a tiered cascade, reaching ~90% at Tiers 0--3. That
cascade and this offset fit are *the same procedure at different
scales*: both recover a mapping by accumulating independent assertions
until one alignment dominates, and both treat residual ambiguity as a
queue for human adjudication rather than a failure.

Worth writing up as a method rather than twice as an implementation
detail. The NVS globe↔TLN mapping tool is the natural place for the
shared machinery to live.

* 6. Why this belongs in a HAVI report

HAVI's diagnosis is that AI tooling erases "the cultural, material, and
perceptual differences that are essential to humanistic research." Our
three layers are a documented, quantified instance: a unique identity
system deleted without error, citation declarations severed from the
structures they describe, and an entire edition's lineation replaced by
a synthetic count that nobody could check.

Three observations seem worth reporting.

*First, the failure mode is silence.* None of the three layers produced
an error, a warning or a validation failure. The corrective discipline
we have adopted -- a *coverage assertion* requiring that every character
of an entry be accounted for as extracted, consumed, or explicitly
discarded -- is cheap, mechanical, and would have caught Layer 1 in
seconds. It is tokenizer discipline applied to editorial work.

*Second, the humanistic artifact supplies the ground truth.* Schmidt,
Abbott, Onions and Dyce are not training data and not objects of
analysis here. They function as *instruments*: an assembled body of
verified assertions dense enough to recover information that machine
vision applied to page images could produce only expensively and
unreliably. This inverts the usual arrangement, in which the humanities
supply material and computation supplies method.

*Third, the honest scope of AI assistance.* Large models read printed
pages well enough to be useful, but their failure mode on marginal
line numbers is to produce a plausible number rather than an error --
the wrong shape of mistake for an oracle. The productive division of
labour puts the model where its errors are visible: proposing and
testing hypotheses, writing and running the fitting code, surveying
20,000 entries for regularity, and flagging the cases a person must
adjudicate. Notably, two of the day's substantive corrections went the
other way -- an assistant's inference from a three-line sample was
wrong about the Folger =@n= values, and a scholar's knowledge of the
printed page corrected it.

* 7. Immediate next steps

1. *Re-process the P4 Shakespeare files*, preserving the distinction
   between transcribed and counted numbers. The P5 derivatives are not
   worth repairing; the information needed to build them correctly is
   in =Renaissance/Shakespeare/opensource/= and was discarded in
   transit. Transcribed anchors must survive into the P5 as such --
   flagged in the markup, not merged into an interpolated sequence.
2. Extract Schmidt's /King Lear/ citations (5,072, across 26 scenes)
   with headword and expected inflected form.
3. Reconcile boundaries against anchors; adjudicate the residue with
   the oracle; report confidence per interval.
4. Verify against the Lear page images.
5. Decide Globe retention on the evidence: if the lineation recovers
   cleanly, repair the Globe editions as the citation-bearing texts; if
   it does not, retire them explicitly, with the reasoning recorded in
   the work-level =__cts__.xml= and in =forum.org=.
6. Rebuild the =corpus-tools= globe↔TLN utility on a text-matched
   alignment rather than on =@n=; survey existing Globe↔TLN maps from
   other projects first.
7. Add a corpus-wide Schematron assertion: every declared
   =citeStructure= must match at least one node.
