* 1. The occasion

We set out to clean up the TEI encoding of Schmidt's
/Shakespeare-Lexicon/ (1874; 2nd ed. 1886) so that its citations could
be resolved to CTS URNs in the Minimum Viable Perseus corpus. The
Lexicon holds 20,101 entries and something over 230,000 citations into
the Shakespearean text. It is, as we came to put it, *a database that
happens to have been typeset*: ~20,000 highly regular records, not
prose.

What began as an encoding cleanup turned into a diagnosis of three
nested failures, each one hidden beneath the last, and each invisible
to ordinary validation.

* 2. Three layers of failure

** Layer 1: silent structural loss in migration

The P4 source (=Renaissance/Schmidt/copyright/schmidt.xml=) carries
=<entryFree key="Abate" type="main">= -- 20,101 entries, 20,101 unique
keys, no collisions, with homographs already disambiguated
(=Bate1=/=Bate2=, =A1=--=A7=; 1,922 headwords carry more than one
entry). The P5 derivative on which a year of work had been built has
*no* =@key= and no =@xml:id= whatsoever.

The 2025 corpus-wide P4→P5 migration dropped a complete identity
system. It threw no error. Nothing downstream noticed, because nothing
downstream was checking that the output still contained the input. The
loss was found only because both files happened to be open at once.

** Layer 2: declarations addressing structures that no longer exist

Perseus's TEI files declare their citation schemes in
=<refsDecl>/<citeStructure>=. We found several that address element
patterns which earlier, well-intentioned cleanup passes had dismantled:

- =ven.xml= declared =<citeStructure unit="line" match=".//l[@n]"/>=
  when not one of its 1,196 =<l>= elements carried =@n=. The
  declaration matched nothing at all.
- =luc.xml= carried correct global line numbers on =<lb n="704">=
  milestones while declaring stanza-relative position, leaving those
  numbers unaddressable.
- =pp.xml=, after a repair pass, numbered lines restarting within each
  of its twenty poems -- while Schmidt cites /The Passionate Pilgrim/
  by continuous line number up to 430. Three-quarters of his 995
  =Pilgr.= citations became unreachable.
- Every play file still declares its line level as
  =match=".//lb[@ed='G']" use="position()"=, though an earlier
  =strip-lb= pass moved Globe numbers onto =<l @n>= and left =<lb>=
  only in prose. /King Lear/ offers 65 addressable =<lb>= against 2,550
  numbered lines.

A declared citeStructure that matches zero or near-zero nodes is a
defect class worth asserting against corpus-wide. It is the exact
analogue, at the level of declaration, of the coverage failure at Layer
1: a claim about structure that nothing verifies.

** Layer 3: the line numbers themselves are synthetic

This is the serious one.

Schmidt cites /Lr./ II, 4, 161 for =abate=. In the printed Globe
edition (verified against the Internet Archive's digitisation, p. 860)
line 161 is *She hath abated me of half my train*. Schmidt is correct.

In our P5 Globe file that line is numbered 162. In the Folger/DraCor
edition it is =<l xml:id="ftln-1539" n="179">=. Three numbers for one
line.

The P4 encoding explains why. Greg's original markup reads:

#+BEGIN_SRC xml
<l>She hath abated me of half my train; <lb ed="G"/><lb n="1442" ed="F1"/></l>
#+END_SRC

The F1 through-line numbers were transcribed densely and carry =@n=.
The Globe line *boundaries* were marked, but their *numbers* were not.
Only the sparse printed decade markers survive as numbered =<lb ed="G"
n="…">= -- and in /King Lear/ all 65 of those sit inside =<p>=
elements. **In verse there are no anchors at all.** Every Globe line
number in our corpus was produced by counting boundaries, with nothing
to check the count against.

The count is wrong, and wrong differently in each scene:

| Location | Printed Globe | Perseus P5 | Offset |
|----------+---------------+------------+--------|
| I.1 "Meantime we shall express our darker purpose." | 37 | 38 | +1 |
| I.1 "To shake all cares and business from our age;" | 40 | 41 | +1 |
| I.1 "Since now we will divest us, both of rule," | 50 | 51 | +1 |
| I.1 "As much as child e'er loved, or father found;" | 60 | 61 | +1 |
| II.4 "With how depraved a quality--O Regan!" | 139 | 140 | −1 |
| II.4 "Nature in you stands on the very verge" | 149 | 150 | −1 |
| II.4 "[Rising] Never, Regan:" | 160 | 161 | +1 |

In I.1 the drift enters before the verse begins -- most plausibly from
counting the =Sennet. Enter KING LEAR…= stage direction, which the
Globe does not number -- and then persists, unchecked, for 286 lines.
In II.4 the offset is −1 and then flips to +1 at a *duplicated* number:
two distinct lines both numbered 160, neither carrying =@part=. Across
/King Lear/ there are 22 such collisions in 12 of its 26 scenes.

Duplicate numbers break CTS addressing outright, independently of
alignment: =lr.globe:2.4.160= is ambiguous today.

* 3. The underlying problem: citations are edition-bound

The three layers share a root. Each is a case of edition-specific
material being flattened into an apparently uniform representation, and
the flattening leaving no trace of itself.

The clearest instance is textual rather than numerical. At Globe I.1
line 150 the Globe reads *Of her confine*; the Folger text reads *Of
his confine*. The same nominal location in two editions of equal
standing carries different words. A citation resolved from one lineation
onto the other can land on a line that does not contain the word being
illustrated -- and will do so silently.

This is the practical face of a theoretical problem already identified
in the NVS work: **CTS URNs model a work as a single citable object,
and cannot represent works with competing textual traditions of equal
authority.** /Lr./ II, 4, 161 is not a coordinate in /King Lear/. It is
a coordinate in one printing of one edition. Schmidt is an unusually
good witness for the argument precisely because he is rigorous,
exhaustive and 150 years old: 233,000 citations that are exact,
verifiable, and unresolvable in the abstract.

Schmidt's Lexicon is not a text about Shakespeare that happens to
contain references. It is an index into a specific material artifact.
Digitisation that discards which artifact has discarded the thing that
makes the index work.

* 4. The proposed method: reference works as alignment oracles

Recovering the Globe lineation by transcription would mean reading
page images for 37 plays. We propose instead to recover it from
evidence we already hold.

** The idea

A reference work that cites densely into a text is a large collection
of *independent word-location assertions*. Schmidt asserts that
=abate= (in the form /abated/) occurs at /Lr./ II, 4, 161. That is a
falsifiable claim about our text, testable without human judgement.

Given a text whose lineation is uncertain by a small unknown offset,
and a few hundred such assertions per scene, the offset can be
recovered by fitting: for each (work, act, scene), find the integer δ
maximising the proportion of citations whose headword occurs at line
/n/+δ. The reference work becomes an *alignment oracle*.

The dependency looks circular and is not. We are not using Schmidt to
decide what the lines say; the text is fixed and known. We are using
thousands of independent assertions to recover a single small integer
per scene.

** Why reference works make good oracles

- *Density.* Schmidt yields 96,704 act/scene/line citations across the
  plays. /Hamlet/ alone has 6,153; /King Lear/ 5,072 across 26 scenes,
  median 136 citations per scene.
- *Independence.* Each citation was made separately, by a scholar
  consulting a page. Errors are uncorrelated; a systematic offset in our
  data is not.
- *Verifiability.* The claim is a string match, not an interpretation.
  Schmidt's own headword-elision conventions (=--d=, =a.=) tell us
  which inflected form to expect.
- *Redundancy across witnesses.* Abbott's /Shakespearian Grammar/,
  Onions's /Glossary/ and Dyce's /Glossary/ all cite Globe lineation
  independently. Agreement across four nineteenth-century reference
  works is strong evidence; disagreement localises a problem.

** Self-diagnosis

The method reports its own confidence, which is its chief virtue. A
scene whose citations peak sharply at a single δ has a constant offset
and is solved. A scene with no clear peak has an internal duplicate or
gap rather than a uniform shift -- which is precisely the scene whose
page image needs consulting. The fit thus reduces a 37-play
transcription problem to a short list of flagged scenes.

Known weak spots: /King Lear/'s smallest scene carries only 12
citations, and short scenes will not fit reliably. Prose passages,
where Globe numbers printed lines rather than verse lines, need
separate handling.

** Validation plan

1. Check first whether the NVS globe↔TLN mapping tool already carries a
   verified Globe lineation for /King Lear/. If it does, this is a
   join, not a project. (This check is required by the NVS
   collaboration protocol, added after an earlier episode in which days
   were spent on an elaborate solution to a problem already solved in
   the source.)
2. Fit offsets for /King Lear/ from Schmidt alone.
3. Verify against the Internet Archive page images already downloaded.
4. Repeat with Abbott, Onions and Dyce; measure agreement.
5. Only then generalise to the remaining plays.

* 5. Relation to the NVS work

The Hypervariorum NVS project has been resolving Furness's /King Lear/
bibliography through a tiered cascade, reaching ~90% at Tiers 0--3. That
cascade and this offset fit are *the same procedure at different
scales*: both recover a mapping by accumulating independent assertions
until one alignment dominates, and both treat residual ambiguity as a
queue for human adjudication rather than a failure.

Worth writing up as a method rather than twice as an implementation
detail. The NVS globe↔TLN mapping tool is the natural place for the
shared machinery to live.

* 6. Why this belongs in a HAVI report

HAVI's diagnosis is that AI tooling erases "the cultural, material, and
perceptual differences that are essential to humanistic research." Our
three layers are a documented, quantified instance: a unique identity
system deleted without error, citation declarations severed from the
structures they describe, and an entire edition's lineation replaced by
a synthetic count that nobody could check.

Three observations seem worth reporting.

*First, the failure mode is silence.* None of the three layers produced
an error, a warning or a validation failure. The corrective discipline
we have adopted -- a *coverage assertion* requiring that every character
of an entry be accounted for as extracted, consumed, or explicitly
discarded -- is cheap, mechanical, and would have caught Layer 1 in
seconds. It is tokenizer discipline applied to editorial work.

*Second, the humanistic artifact supplies the ground truth.* Schmidt,
Abbott, Onions and Dyce are not training data and not objects of
analysis here. They function as *instruments*: an assembled body of
verified assertions dense enough to recover information that machine
vision applied to page images could produce only expensively and
unreliably. This inverts the usual arrangement, in which the humanities
supply material and computation supplies method.

*Third, the honest scope of AI assistance.* Large models read printed
pages well enough to be useful, but their failure mode on marginal
line numbers is to produce a plausible number rather than an error --
the wrong shape of mistake for an oracle. The productive division of
labour puts the model where its errors are visible: proposing and
testing hypotheses, writing and running the fitting code, surveying
20,000 entries for regularity, and flagging the cases a person must
adjudicate. Notably, two of the day's substantive corrections went the
other way -- an assistant's inference from a three-line sample was
wrong about the Folger =@n= values, and a scholar's knowledge of the
printed page corrected it.

1. Check the NVS globe↔TLN tool before building anything.
2. Extract Schmidt's /King Lear/ citations (5,072, across 26 scenes)

3. Implement per-scene offset fitting; report peak sharpness alongside

4. Verify against the Lear page images.
5. Decide Globe retention on the evidence: if offsets recover cleanly,

6. Add a corpus-wide Schematron assertion: every declared
   =citeStructure= must match at least one node.
