* Purpose

Perseus's original Shakespeare corpus (Globe edition, encoded ~2006) is being
revisited as part of a new effort to build a Perseus-style reading
environment for Shakespeare -- readable text, tokenized and linked to
linguistic analysis, with links to dictionaries and commentaries, comparable
to Perseus's existing environment for Greek and Latin.

This document compares four candidate sources of encoded Shakespeare text --
the existing Perseus encoding, the Folger Shakespeare Library's digital
texts, the Text Creation Partnership's EEBO transcriptions, and Internet
Shakespeare Editions -- as possible foundations for that work.

* Characteristics of Available Editions

** Perseus (original Globe edition encoding)

*** Source text
The 1866 Globe edition (Clark & Wright), out of copyright. This is the same
base edition used by, e.g., OpenSourceShakespeare.

*** Encoding quality
Encoded roughly twenty years ago under Perseus Digital Library conventions
of that period: structural TEI markup for acts/scenes/speeches with
canonical citation, but -- consistent with Perseus practice of that era
generally -- almost certainly *not* tokenized at the word level, and without
an embedded citation scheme tying individual lines to any alternative
numbering system (e.g., Folio TLN). Should be confirmed directly against
the original files rather than assumed here.

*** Scholarly use
Perseus Digital Library is one of the most heavily used digital humanities
resources in existence -- but overwhelmingly for its Greek and Latin
holdings. Its Shakespeare/English Renaissance materials are a much smaller
part of its footprint and are not a standard citation in Shakespeare
studies the way Folger, Arden, or ISE editions are.

*** Licensing
Perseus's own licensing is mixed and has evolved over time: public-domain
source texts have historically been released under CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 US;
newer Perseus components (annotations, derived datasets) trend toward CC
BY-SA 4.0 (no NC clause). Worth confirming which license actually applies
to the specific Shakespeare files in question.

** Folger Shakespeare Library digital texts (Folger Digital Texts)

*** Source text
The New Folger Shakespeare: a modern-spelling critical edition, edited by
Folger's own editorial team from Folio/Quarto sources. Not diplomatic --
spelling, punctuation, and some lineation are editorially normalized.

*** Encoding quality
The strongest of the four by a clear margin. TEI XML with genuine
word-level tokenization: every word, space, and punctuation mark has its
own =xml:id= (=<w>=, =<c>=, =<pc>=). Every line carries a =milestone=
element (=unit="ftln"=) giving both a Folger-specific through-line number
and, directly as an attribute, the Globe-style act.scene.line citation
(=n="1.1.1"=), plus verse/prose type and =prev=/=next= links for
reconstructing split verse lines. Most named characters resolve to full
names via a =<listPerson>= in the header; anonymous/chorus roles (Witches,
Murderers, Apparitions) are only given numbered IDs with no individual
names encoded. Actively maintained; bulk XML download available for all 37
plays as static files (no scraping required).

*** Scholarly use
Very widely used as a reading text, especially in education and by digital
tools that need a free, modern-spelling Shakespeare source. The Folger
Shakespeare Library is a leading institution, though for textual/editorial
scholarship specifically, Folger's edition is one voice among several
(Arden, Oxford, RSC, ISE), not a singular standard.

*** Licensing
CC BY-NC 3.0 (Attribution-NonCommercial), confirmed directly in the XML
header (=<licence target="http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/3.0/...">=).
Non-commercial use only; commercial use would require separate permission.

** EEBO-TCP (Text Creation Partnership, now hosted via University of
   Michigan)

*** Source text
A diplomatic, old-spelling transcription of the actual 1623 First Folio
(TCP id =A11954=), double-keyed by the Text Creation Partnership rather
than OCR'd. Not an edited reading text -- original spelling, capitalization,
and typography (including the long s) are preserved as printed.

*** Encoding quality
Structurally the lightest of the four. TEI markup at the =<l>=/=<p>=
(line/paragraph) level, not tokenized to the word. Speaker tags use the
literal Folio speech-prefixes as printed (=<speaker>1.</speaker>=,
=<speaker>King.</speaker>=), with no resolved character identity or ID to
look one up by. Page breaks are marked with the original Folio page number
and a facsimile-image reference (=<pb n="131" facs="tcp:11596:372"/>=), but
there is no line-, column-, or through-line-level numbering of any kind --
neither Hinman's TLN nor a modern act.scene.line citation.

*** Scholarly use
Extremely widely used as a foundational corpus in digital humanities and
historical-English corpus linguistics generally (it underlies many
downstream projects, e.g. EarlyPrint). For Shakespeare specifically it is
used more by bibliographers and textual/historical-linguistics scholars
than by literary scholars seeking a citable reading edition.

*** Licensing
CC0 1.0 Universal -- full public-domain dedication, no restrictions
whatsoever, not even attribution. Phase 1 texts were freed in 2015; Phase 2
(which includes this Folio transcription) was freed on 2020-08-01.

** Internet Shakespeare Editions (ISE, University of Victoria)

*** Source text
Multiple parallel editions per play: old-spelling diplomatic transcriptions
of specific Quarto/Folio witnesses, plus a modern-spelling critical edition
prepared by ISE's own scholarly editors (each play individually edited and
attributed, generally peer-reviewed).

*** Encoding quality
High scholarly quality, with one feature unique among these four sources:
ISE's modern-edition pages tag *every line* with both a Globe-style
act.scene.line number and Hinman's First Folio through-line number (TLN)
together -- directly solving the Folio-TLN-to-Globe-line correlation that
motivated this whole inquiry. Verified directly by scraping ISE's Macbeth
pages: e.g. TLN 98 = line 1.3.1 = "Where hast thou been, sister?". However,
this data is only available as rendered HTML (line milestones as anchor
tags), not as a bulk XML/TEI download -- extracting it correctly required
reverse-engineering several undocumented rendering conventions (split
verse lines, mid-line speaker interruptions, multiple TLNs embedded within
a single modern line).

*** Scholarly use
Well-established (founded 1999) and specifically respected within
Shakespeare studies, since -- unlike Folger, TCP, or Perseus -- it exists
solely to produce peer-reviewed scholarly editions of Shakespeare with
named textual editors per play.

*** Licensing
"Educational, non-profit purposes" explicitly permitted; commercial/other
use requires negotiation with the rights holder (the individual editor, or
ISE itself for unedited texts). Similar restrictive character to Folger and
Perseus's NC-licensed materials; not CC0.

* Comparison Summary

| Edition  | Text type              | Tokenization  | Citation scheme               | License          |
|----------+-------------------------+---------------+--------------------------------+-------------------|
| Perseus  | Modernized (Globe 1866) | Line-level    | Canonical, no TLN link         | CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 (mixed w/ newer CC BY-SA 4.0 components) |
| Folger   | Modernized (edited)     | Word-level    | FTLN + Globe act.scene.line    | CC BY-NC 3.0     |
| EEBO-TCP | Diplomatic (Folio 1623) | Line-level    | None (page breaks only)        | CC0 1.0          |
| ISE      | Both (diplomatic + modernized) | Line-level | Hinman TLN + Globe act.scene.line | Educational/non-profit only |

* Pros and Cons as a Basis for the New Perseus Work

** Perseus (retain and upgrade the existing encoding)

*** Pros
- No licensing renegotiation needed; Perseus already controls (most of) the
  rights to its own materials.
- Continuity with twenty years of existing citations, links, and any
  downstream tools built against the current URNs/structure.
- Full control over encoding decisions going forward.

*** Cons
- Requires redoing essentially all of the architectural work Folger has
  already done: word-level tokenization, a coherent citation/milestone
  scheme, split-line handling.
- The existing encoding's actual quality is unverified in this review and
  should be audited before committing to build on it.
- Reinvents infrastructure other projects have already built and
  maintained for years.

** Folger Digital Texts

*** Pros
- By far the best engineering to build on: word-level IDs are exactly the
  granularity needed to attach morphological/linguistic annotation, and the
  Globe-style citation is already embedded per line.
- Actively maintained, bulk-downloadable, consistent across all 37 plays.
- Would dramatically cut development time compared to encoding from
  scratch.

*** Cons
- CC BY-NC 3.0 licensing conflicts with Perseus's historical goal of
  maximal reuse (including by downstream commercial tools/mirrors); any
  corpus built on it inherits the NC restriction and cannot be relicensed
  more permissively.
- It's a modernized edition, not the original-spelling historical document
  -- a different scholarly artifact than what bibliographically-minded
  users may want.
- Anonymous/chorus role names (Witches, Murderers, etc.) are not resolved
  in the source data and would need to be supplied from elsewhere.

** EEBO-TCP

*** Pros
- Fully unencumbered (CC0): the only source here with zero licensing
  constraints on redistribution or commercial use, matching Perseus's
  traditional preference for maximally open materials.
- The actual historical document (the 1623 Folio itself), useful if
  bibliographic/documentary fidelity matters to the project's goals.
- Professionally double-keyed rather than OCR'd, so transcription quality
  is high even though structural markup is light.

*** Cons
- Least processed of the four: no word tokenization, no line-numbering
  scheme of any kind (not even a reconstructable one), and speaker
  identity is just the raw printed abbreviation with no resolvable ID.
- Old-spelling text is a harder base for general-purpose linguistic
  tooling (lemmatization, dictionary linking) without significant
  additional normalization work.
- Adopting it means taking on effectively the same scope of original
  encoding work as updating the existing Perseus corpus, just from a
  better-proofread starting text.

** Internet Shakespeare Editions

*** Pros
- Only source that already solves the original Hinman-TLN-to-Globe-line
  correlation problem directly, per line, for both diplomatic and modern
  editions.
- Genuine peer-reviewed scholarly editing per play, specific to
  Shakespeare, by named textual editors.
- Offers both a diplomatic and a modernized edition of most plays side by
  side, which could support both reading and bibliographic use cases.

*** Cons
- No bulk data export; everything must be scraped from rendered HTML, and
  the rendering conventions (split lines, mid-line speaker changes,
  multiple TLNs per modern line) are undocumented and had to be
  reverse-engineered empirically in this review -- a fragile foundation to
  build 37 plays' worth of tooling on.
- Licensing is non-commercial/educational only, same restriction class as
  Folger.
- Not word-tokenized; would still require building out linguistic-analysis
  infrastructure from line-level text.

* Open Questions for Discussion

- Does the new Perseus environment need to support commercial/unrestricted
  downstream reuse, or is non-commercial academic use sufficient? This is
  the single factor that most determines whether Folger/ISE are viable at
  all.
- Is bibliographic fidelity to the First Folio itself a project goal, or is
  a modernized reading text (Globe- or Folger-style) sufficient?
- Is the Hinman TLN correlation (the original motivating question) actually
  a requirement for the new reading environment, or was it specific to a
  separate lookup-file use case?
- Could Folger's *structure* (word-level IDs, milestone citation scheme) be
  reimplemented over the CC0 EEBO-TCP text, combining the best encoding
  design with the most open license?
