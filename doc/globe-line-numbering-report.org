#+TITLE: Globe Line-Number Interpolation: Editorial Review Report
#+DATE: 2026-08-11
#+AUTHOR: Clifford Wulfman
#+OPTIONS: toc:t num:nil

* Overview

This report documents every place the automated Globe line-numbering
pass could not simply and unambiguously derive a correct =@n= value,
and needs a classicist's eye before it's treated as authoritative.

=corpus-tools='s =number-verse-lines= command (branch
=number-verse-lines=, commit =4430e36=, merged to =main= at =07f0587=)
was run over all 37 in-scope Shakespeare plays in
=canonical-engLit/data/shakespeare/=, per the algorithm in
=src/mvp/number_verse_lines_spec.md=. It interpolates the Globe
scene-relative line number onto every =<l>= that lacks one, using the
=@n= anchors already present (transferred earlier from =<lb ed="G">= by
=strip-lb-tags.xsl=) as ground truth to interpolate between. The result
is committed at =bff1e44=: 66,290 of 66,292 =<l>= elements now carry
=@n=.

Interpolation is exact when the printed Globe apparatus is intact: two
anchors 10 lines apart with 9 unnumbered lines between them get 1--9
added cleanly. This report is about the cases where that isn't true —
where the count of lines between two anchors doesn't match the numeric
gap between them, where the anchors themselves contradict each other,
or where there was nothing to interpolate from at all. In every case
below the script did *something* reasonable and deterministic (never
silently dropping data, never touching an anchor), but "reasonable and
deterministic" is not the same as "correct," and these are the places
where only checking against a printed Globe edition will resolve it.

"Collision" means: the number of unnumbered =<l>= elements between two
anchors is greater than or equal to the numeric gap between those
anchors, so there isn't room to give each one a distinct integer
without touching an anchor's own value. The script's fallback is to
number them as evenly as it can and let the last one or two tie an
anchor rather than skip past it — see "How the script handles a
collision" below.

* Priority 1: likely source transcription errors (7 cases)

These are not ordinary "too many lines for the gap" collisions — in
each of these, the *second* anchor's value is lower than (or, in one
case, identical to) the first, meaning the Globe line numbers in the
source actually go backwards (or repeat) within a single scene. That
can't be explained by verse density; it's much more likely a
transcription error in the historical Globe-line-number data (a
mistyped digit, a misplaced =<lb>=, or similar), inherited from
whatever process originally captured the Globe apparatus for this
corpus. Two of the seven are especially telling:

- *Hamlet* 5.2: anchor 370 is "Which have solicited. The rest is
  silence." — Hamlet's dying line, one of the most famous exits in the
  play — followed immediately by anchor 330 on "And our affairs from
  England come too late:". The second number is very likely a
  transposed digit for something in the 430s, not 330.
- *Richard II* 2.1: anchor 301 is followed by anchor 41 on "This royal
  throne of kings, this scepter'd isle," — John of Gaunt's "this
  England" speech, one of the best-known passages in the play, which
  in most editions *is* numbered around line 40. That strongly
  suggests 41 is the correct number and 301 is the error, not the
  reverse.

*Romeo and Juliet* 3.3 has a different flavor of the same problem: two
different lines both carry anchor value 50 (a straightforward
duplicate, not a reversal), leaving zero room for the 9 unnumbered
lines between them.

*Recommendation:* check each of these seven against a printed Globe
edition (or another digitized Globe apparatus) and correct the
offending =@n= value directly on the =<l>= in question. Because
=strip-lb-tags.xsl= already removed the original =<lb ed="G">=
milestones once they were transferred to =@n=, there's no surviving
=<lb>= to cross-check against in this corpus — the fix has to go
straight onto the =<l>='s =@n=.

* Priority 2: moderate numbering collisions (78 cases)

These are ordinary collisions — more unnumbered lines between two
(correctly ordered, non-duplicate) anchors than the numeric gap allows
for — but by more than the unavoidable single-line boundary case (P3,
below). An overshoot of 1 means one interpolated line has to share a
number with its neighbor (or the anchor); an overshoot of 7 (the worst
in this batch, =A Midsummer Night's Dream= 4.1) means several do.

Coriolanus, Cymbeline, and Henry VIII account for over half of this
tier between them (8, 12, and 10 cases respectively) — worth a look as
a group, since a systematic pattern in one play (e.g. a run of scenes
where the Globe apparatus was sparser than usual) is more likely to be
a real edition-numbering-density issue than 78 independent
transcription slips.

* Priority 3: boundary-tie collisions (382 cases, lowest priority)

This is the largest category by far, and the least concerning: it's
the case where the number of unnumbered lines between two anchors
exactly equals the numeric gap (e.g. 9 lines between anchors 10 lines
apart). There are only =gap - 1= integers strictly between two anchors,
so this is *always* one line short — mechanically unavoidable, not a
sign of anything wrong with the source. The practical effect is that
exactly one interpolated line ties the following anchor's value rather
than getting a distinct number.

* Plays with no Globe-anchor grounding at all (9 plays)

For these plays, *no scene has a single =@n= anchor* — every line
number the script produced is a bare sequential count starting at 1
within its scene, with no relationship to the historical Globe
printing whatsoever. This is different from every category above: it's
not a case of the algorithm compressing or guessing around sparse
data, it's a case of having *no* data to work from at all.

=per.xml= and =tro.xml= were already flagged in this project's
=CLAUDE.md= as under-encoded and out of scope for the current MVP
sprint, so their appearance here isn't new. The other seven *are* new:
nothing previously documented that these five histories and two
comedies carry zero Globe apparatus in this corpus. Two possibilities
worth distinguishing before deciding what to do about it:

1. The original Renaissance-derived source genuinely never captured
   periodic Globe line numbers for these seven plays (i.e. they're as
   under-encoded as =tro=/=per=, just not previously flagged as such).
2. The Globe numbers exist in some other source (a different Perseus
   corpus generation, or the print edition itself) and could be
   re-derived, but weren't captured in whatever process produced this
   corpus's =Renaissance/= source.

*Recommendation:* until one of these is confirmed, treat every =@n=
value in these nine files as a placeholder position count, not a Globe
citation — they're internally consistent (strictly increasing per
scene) but carry no external authority.

* Unnumbered lines: prose-to-verse transition gap (2 lines)

Two lines were left without =@n= entirely, rather than guessed at,
because there was no preceding new-line element in scope for them to
inherit a number from:

- *Cymbeline* 1.1: "None but the king?" (=Sec. Gent.=, =@part="F"=) is
  the first =<l>= in the scene; everything before it is prose. The
  matching =@part="I"= fragment that should open this shared verse
  line either isn't tagged, or is embedded in the preceding prose
  =<p>=.
- *The Winter's Tale* 1.2: "Stay your thanks a while;" (=Leon.=,
  =@part="F"=), same pattern — immediately followed by =<l n="10"
  part="I">And pay them when you part.</l>=, so the anchor for this
  shared line exists just two elements later; it's specifically the
  "F" half at the very start of the scene that has nothing to attach
  to.

Both are genuine prose→verse transition markup gaps in the source, not
introduced by this pass. *Recommendation:* for the Winter's Tale case
in particular, since =n="10"= appears immediately after on the =I=
fragment, it may be reasonable to simply assign the orphaned =F=
fragment the same =n="10"= by hand — but that's an editorial judgment
call, not something the script should guess at automatically.

* Malformed anchor data left untouched (1 case)

*Coriolanus* 2.1 has one =<l n="131 132">= — a long prose passage
(Menenius's "A letter for me!" speech) mistagged as =<l>= rather than
=<p>=, carrying two space-separated Globe numbers instead of one. The
script leaves any existing =@n= untouched by design (never overwrites
ground truth), so this value is exactly as it was before the pass; it
was excluded from the interpolation math (it can't be parsed as an
integer) and flagged rather than causing a crash.

*Recommendation:* this needs two fixes, not one — the encoding
(=<l>= → =<p>=, matching the rest of the passage's prose siblings) and
the =@n= value itself (pick one of 131/132, or verify against a Globe
edition whether the passage really spans both).

* How the script handles a collision

For readers checking this report against the corpus: when the number
of unnumbered lines between two anchors is greater than or equal to
the numeric gap between them, the script still assigns strictly
increasing integers to as many
of them as it can — evenly spaced, rounded to the nearest integer —
and only lets a value tie (never exceed by more than necessary) the
next anchor if there's truly no room left. It never modifies an
anchor's own =@n=, and it never leaves a normal (non-orphaned)
unnumbered line without *some* value. That's what makes the P2/P3
distinction above meaningful: P3 is the single-tie case that's
mechanically guaranteed by the math whenever =lines between == gap=;
P2 is everything worse than that, which is more likely to reflect a
real problem in the source's Globe apparatus (missing anchors, a
misplaced =<lb>=, or genuinely denser verse than the printed
line-number markers suggest) rather than an artifact of the boundary
condition.
